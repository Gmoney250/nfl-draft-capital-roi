# The NFL's Most Expensive Pick Is Usually the Worst Investment

Here's something that sounds crazy until you look at the data: the top 5 picks in the NFL draft are, dollar for dollar, the WORST investments in the entire draft.

Not the worst players. The worst investments.

I analyzed 61 players drafted between 2013 and 2024 and compared what teams paid for each pick against what they actually got back in production. I measured production using a stat called Approximate Value, or AV — basically a single number that captures everything a player does on the field across their career.

Then I divided AV by salary to get the key metric: **how much production did you get for every million dollars you spent?**

The results are brutal for top picks.

Top 5 picks averaged **0.71** units of production per million dollars. Round 4 picks? **12.29**. That's not a small difference. Late-round picks delivered roughly 17 times more value per dollar than the most expensive picks in the draft.

The quarterback position is where this gets truly insane. Dak Prescott, drafted in the 4th round with the 135th overall pick, put up 19.43 AV per million. Caleb Williams, the #1 overall pick, put up 0.35. That's a **55x gap in efficiency**. Brock Purdy — Mr. Irrelevant, the literal last pick in his draft — put up more value per dollar than almost every top-5 QB in the sample.

So what's going on? Why do smart NFL teams keep paying billions of dollars for picks that produce less value per dollar?

The answer is the same reason people buy expensive handbags, Ivy League degrees, and overpriced stocks: **certainty costs extra**. Top picks have a 61.5% "hit rate" — the percentage that become solid starters. Late-round picks hit much less often. So teams pay a massive premium for the LIKELIHOOD of getting a useful player, even though the EXPECTED VALUE per dollar is much worse.

This is the same mistake investors make with blue-chip stocks. You pay extra for the feeling of safety. You pay less for the unknown. But the unknown, on average, delivers better returns once you spread your bets across enough of them.

The NFL lesson applies everywhere. Hiring the Harvard MBA feels safer than hiring the state school kid with great work. Buying NVIDIA feels safer than the smaller chip company with better fundamentals. Drafting the star QB from Alabama feels safer than the 4th-rounder from Mississippi State.

Sometimes the expensive pick is worth it. But not as often as the price tag implies.

The teams that win in the NFL long-term aren't the ones with the most top-5 picks. They're the ones who consistently find value in rounds 3-7 while avoiding disaster up top. The Patriots built a dynasty this way. The 49ers are doing it right now with Brock Purdy. The Eagles did it with guys like Jason Kelce (63rd pick) and Lane Johnson (4th round).

Expensive isn't always smart. Usually, the bargain is the smart pick — you just have to be brave enough to bet on the unproven.
